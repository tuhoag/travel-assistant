# Rome

## Overview

Rome is the capital of Italy and, according to legend, was founded in 753 BC by Romulus on the banks of the Tiber River. It grew from a small city-state into the center of the Roman Kingdom, Republic, and Empire, which at its height controlled territory spanning from Britain to the Middle East. The modern city covers about 1,285 square kilometers and has a population of roughly 2.8 million people, making it the largest and most populous municipality in Italy.

Rome is often referred to as the "Eternal City," a name reflecting the belief, held since antiquity, that the city would endure forever regardless of the rise and fall of empires around it. Its historic center, a dense layering of ancient, medieval, and Renaissance architecture, was one of the first sites designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site, in 1980, alongside the extraterritorial properties of the Holy See within its boundaries.

The city has served as a capital twice in its history: first as the seat of the Roman Empire in antiquity, and again since 1871, when it became the capital of the newly unified Kingdom of Italy, a status it retained through the founding of the modern Italian Republic in 1946.

## Culture and Landmarks

As Italy's political and cultural capital, Rome hosts the country's national government, including the Quirinal Palace, home to the President of Italy, and the Palazzo Montecitorio, seat of the Chamber of Deputies. The city is also home to Vatican City, an independent city-state entirely enclosed within Rome that serves as the spiritual center of the Roman Catholic Church and residence of the Pope.

Rome's archaeological and artistic heritage is unmatched in scale, encompassing landmarks such as the Colosseum, the Roman Forum, and the Pantheon, alongside Renaissance and Baroque treasures like the Trevi Fountain and St. Peter's Basilica. Millions of visitors travel to the city each year specifically to see these sites, making tourism one of its largest industries.

Beyond its ancient ruins, Rome remains a major center for fashion, cinema, and cuisine, having played a formative role in Italian film through the Cinecittà studios, sometimes called the "Hollywood on the Tiber." The city's culinary traditions, including dishes such as carbonara and cacio e pepe, have become internationally recognized symbols of Roman and Italian culture more broadly.
